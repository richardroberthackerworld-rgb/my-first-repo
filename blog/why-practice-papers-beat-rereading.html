Why Practice Papers Beat Re-Reading (The Testing Effect)

July 14, 2026 · 9 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

Cognitive science has a clear, well-replicated answer on the best use of a revision hour — and it is not reading the chapter again. If you take one idea from this article into your next exam season, make it this one, because it quietly determines how much of your study time turns into marks.

Almost everyone revises by re-reading. You go over your notes, then over them again, highlighting a bit more each pass, until the words feel comfortable and known. It feels like the responsible thing to do. And it is one of the least efficient ways to prepare for an exam that has ever been studied. The alternative — testing yourself instead of re-reading — is not a marginal improvement; in study after study it produces dramatically better recall for the same time spent. Understanding why is what makes you willing to actually change your habits.

The illusion of fluency

Re-reading feels productive for a specific, seductive reason: the material gets more familiar each time, and your brain mistakes that growing familiarity for growing knowledge. The second pass is easier than the first, the third easier still, and that sense of ease registers as "I know this now." Psychologists call it the illusion of fluency, and it is one of the most expensive mistakes in all of studying.

The problem is that familiarity and recall are two different things. Re-reading trains you to recognise the material — to nod along when you see it. But in the exam hall, nobody hands you the page to recognise. You are given a blank sheet and asked to produce the answer from nothing. Recognition and recall are separate mental muscles, and re-reading builds only the weaker, less-tested one. This is exactly why so many students walk out of an exam bewildered: "I knew that chapter, I read it four times." They did know it, in the recognition sense. They just never trained the muscle the exam actually uses.

The testing effect

The remedy has a name — the testing effect, sometimes called retrieval practice — and it is one of the most robust findings in learning science, confirmed across decades and hundreds of studies. The core result is simple: the act of trying to pull information out of your memory strengthens that memory far more than being re-exposed to the same information. Struggling to recall something, even imperfectly, does more for retention than smoothly reading it again.

The numbers are striking. In one much-cited experiment, students who tested themselves on material retained roughly 50% more of it a week later than students who spent the same amount of time re-reading. Same hours, half again as much remembered — simply by changing what they did with the hours. And here is the part that surprises people most: getting an answer wrong still helps, provided you then see the correct one. A mistake you make and correct is remembered better than a fact you were never tested on at all, because the failed retrieval attempt primes your brain to lock in the correction. This is why you should welcome wrong answers in practice rather than fear them — each one is a memory being strengthened, not a sign of failure.

Why retrieval works

It helps to know the mechanism, because it makes the habit stick. Every time you successfully haul a fact up from memory, you strengthen and slightly rebuild the neural path to it, making it easier to find next time. Re-reading lays down no such path — the information simply arrives at your eyes again, requiring no effort to retrieve, and effortless input produces weak storage. Memory rewards the struggle of retrieval precisely because that struggle is the signal that this is worth keeping. Testing yourself is, quite literally, the brain-level act of exam preparation; re-reading only feels like it.

How to use this tomorrow

None of this helps unless it changes what you do at your desk, so here is the concrete routine.

  1. Finish a chapter once, normally — read it, understand it, take your notes. You do need a first exposure; retrieval practice replaces the endless re-reading, not the initial learning.
  2. Now close the book and take a fresh question paper on that exact chapter. This used to be the hard part — where do you find questions on demand? — but 7Marks generates a full paper with an answer key on any topic in seconds, so the obstacle is gone.
  3. Mark yourself honestly against the key. Do not skim past the ones you were "nearly right" on. Every red mark is not a failure; it is a precise, personalised map of exactly what to restudy — information you simply cannot get from re-reading.
  4. Restudy only the gaps the paper exposed, and then, two days later, take a second, different paper on the same chapter. Because it is freshly generated, you are recalling the material rather than remembering last time's questions.

Space it out

Retrieval practice has a natural partner that multiplies its effect: spacing. Instead of testing yourself on a chapter five times in one evening, spread those tests out — day 1, day 3, day 7, day 21. Each time you successfully recall the material after a longer gap, you roughly double how long the memory will now last. The gaps are not wasted time; the slight forgetting between sessions is what makes the next retrieval effortful, and the effort is the whole point.

This is also the quiet demolition of cramming. A single heroic night before the exam gives you a lot of retrieval in one burst, but no spacing, so the memory is shallow and fades fast — often before you have even finished the paper. A little testing, spread across weeks, costs less total effort and produces recall that actually survives to exam day. Spread beats sprint, every time.

The practical summary

The whole of this article compresses to five words: read once, test many times. Your marks in the exam come from retrieval — from producing answers on a blank page — so that is the skill your revision hours should be building. Every hour you spend re-reading is an hour spent training recognition for an exam that tests recall. Shift those hours to self-testing, space them out, and treat your wrong answers as gifts, and you will remember more, for longer, from less time at the desk. Spend your hours where the marks actually are.
