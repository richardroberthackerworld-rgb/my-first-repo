The Science Behind AI Stem Separation Technology

July 1, 2026 · 9 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

There is a question underneath stem separation that sounds almost philosophical: if two sounds were added together into a single number, how can anything ever pull them apart again? Strictly speaking, it cannot. What actually happens is far more interesting than the marketing word "AI" suggests, and understanding it tells you exactly when to trust the result and when not to.

The problem is unsolvable, technically

Start with the uncomfortable truth. A stereo song is two streams of numbers. At every instant, each number is the sum of everything happening at that moment — the singer, the snare, the bass, the guitar, the reverb tail from three seconds ago.

Ask a mathematician to recover the individual parts from that sum and they will tell you it is underdetermined. You have two equations and perhaps thirty unknowns. There is no unique answer. Infinitely many combinations of instruments produce exactly the sum you are holding.

That is not a limitation of current technology. It is a property of the problem. No future breakthrough will make it exactly solvable.

So how does separation work at all? Because "no unique answer" is not the same as "no good answer". Of the infinitely many possible explanations, almost all of them are absurd — they would require instruments that no human ever played and sounds that no drum ever made. Only a tiny handful are plausible. Separation is the art of finding those.

Sound is more than a waveform

The wiggly line you see in an audio editor is not what an engine analyses. It is far too crude.

Instead, audio is broken into short overlapping slices — typically a few tens of milliseconds each — and each slice is decomposed into the frequencies it contains and how strongly. Stack those slices side by side and you get a spectrogram: a picture of the sound, with time running left to right and frequency bottom to top, brightness showing energy.

This picture is where separation becomes possible, because instruments look strikingly different in it.

A snare drum is a vertical stripe — a burst of energy across nearly all frequencies at once, gone almost immediately. A bass note is a thick horizontal band low down, holding steady. A sung vowel is a set of evenly spaced horizontal lines that drift gently with the singer's pitch, wobbling with vibrato. A cymbal is a bright smear at the top that fades slowly.

Once you see sound this way, "separate the drums" stops being mysterious. It becomes a question of recognising shapes.

Learning what things look like

An engine is not programmed with those descriptions. Nobody sits down and writes a rule saying a snare is a vertical stripe. The rules would be endless and would fail constantly.

Instead it learns from examples. The training material is a large collection of songs where the individual layers were available separately — the actual drum recording, the actual vocal recording, alongside the finished mix.

The engine is shown a mix and asked to produce the separate layers. Its first attempts are meaningless noise. Each attempt is compared against the true answer, the error is measured, and the internal settings are nudged in whatever direction reduces that error. Then again. Millions of times.

Nobody tells it what a snare is. It discovers that a certain shape reliably belongs to the drum layer, because guessing that way reduces its error. Repeat across a vast range of music and it accumulates a working sense of what each instrument family looks like — and, crucially, how they behave across time rather than in a single frozen instant.

Masks: the trick that makes it work

Here is the detail that surprises people. A well-built engine does not usually generate audio from scratch. It generates a filter.

For every point in that time-frequency picture, it estimates a proportion: how much of the energy here belongs to vocals? How much to drums? Think of it as a stencil laid over the spectrogram — mostly opaque where the vocal is absent, mostly transparent where it dominates.

Apply that stencil to the original audio and you get the vocal stem. Apply the inverse and you get everything else.

This matters for a practical reason. Because the output is built from the original recording rather than synthesised, the result keeps the actual texture of the actual performance. It is the real snare, filtered — not a computer's impression of a snare. That is why good separation sounds like a recording rather than like a machine's reconstruction.

It also explains the characteristic failure mode. Where two instruments genuinely overlap — a bass note and a kick drum occupying the same frequency at the same instant — the engine must split that shared energy by proportion. Get it slightly wrong and you hear a faint ghost of one instrument in the other's track. This is why artefacts cluster precisely where instruments collide.

Why the old approach could never get here

Earlier tools took a completely different route: subtract one stereo channel from the other and whatever sat centred cancels out.

That works on a single assumption — that the vocal is centred and other things are not. It is arithmetic, not understanding. And it carries consequences that no refinement can remove: the centred kick and snare vanish along with the singer, the stereo image collapses, the wide reverb tail of the voice survives as a ghost, and a mono recording offers nothing to subtract at all.

Most fundamentally, it has no concept of instruments. It knows only positions. Asking it for a drum track is asking a question in a language it does not speak.

Recognition-based separation replaced it not because it is fashionable but because it answers a different, better question: not where is this sound, but what is this sound.

Overlap, phase, and other awkward realities

A few genuine difficulties are worth knowing, because they explain most disappointing results.

Shared frequencies. When a male vocal, a guitar and a snare all occupy the same region simultaneously, the engine must apportion energy that is genuinely ambiguous. Dense arrangements are harder for exactly this reason.

Phase. The spectrogram captures how much of each frequency is present but discards some information about alignment. Reconstructing audio requires guessing that alignment back. Guess imperfectly and you get a slight watery quality — the artefact people describe as "underwater", and it comes from this step rather than from the recognition itself.

Training bias. An engine learns from what it was shown. Trained mostly on Western pop and rock, it develops confident categories for drum kit, bass guitar, electric guitar and voice. Present it with a sitar, a tabla or an unusual folk instrument and it has no confident slot, so those sounds get distributed into whichever category seems least wrong — usually the catch-all "other" stem.

Compression. Heavily limited modern masters push everything to a similar level, flattening exactly the contrasts the engine uses to tell layers apart.

Why source quality matters so much

This is the single most controllable factor, and it follows directly from everything above.

A lossy encoder achieves small file sizes by discarding detail it judges you will not consciously notice — quiet sounds masked by louder ones, subtle high-frequency content. For casual listening that is a reasonable trade.

For separation it is destructive, because the discarded material is precisely the fine detail that distinguishes one instrument from another. The engine is handed a blurred photograph and asked to identify faces in it.

Feed the same song as a WAV or FLAC and the difference is immediate and obvious. If you want one improvement to your results, this is it — a better source file beats every setting.

How to judge a result properly

Listen to the isolated vocal first, even when what you actually want is the instrumental.

The vocal stem is a diagnostic. If it is clean and complete, the instrumental almost certainly is too. If you hear cymbals or guitar bleeding into it, that same material is now missing from the instrumental, and you have learned that in twenty seconds rather than after a full listen.

Use headphones. Laptop speakers hide everything below roughly 200 Hz, which is exactly where bass and kick separation problems live.

And do not re-process a separated stem hoping to clean it. The engine expects a normal mix as input. Feeding it something already filtered gives it material it was never trained on, and artefacts compound rather than cancel.

Common questions

Is this the same technology as noise removal?

Related, but the goal differs. Noise removal identifies and suppresses unwanted sound; separation identifies several wanted sources and keeps them all, apart. The Noise Remover is tuned for the first job.

Will it ever be perfect?

It will keep improving, but perfect reconstruction is mathematically impossible, not merely difficult. Any tool claiming otherwise is overselling.

Why do results vary so much between songs?

Because difficulty is a property of the mix. Sparse, cleanly produced music separates well; dense, heavily compressed or live material does not. The tool is constant; the songs are not.

Does a longer song separate worse?

No. Processing works through the track in segments, so length affects time and credit cost rather than quality.
