Audio File Formats Explained: MP3 vs WAV vs FLAC

July 1, 2026 · 8 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

Most advice about audio formats is either far too technical or reduces to "use MP3". Neither helps when you are staring at a dropdown wondering whether the choice matters. It does matter, but only at specific moments — and knowing which moments those are is the whole of it.

Lossless and lossy

Every format falls into one of two camps, and this is the only distinction that really matters.

Lossless formats keep every sample of the original recording. WAV stores it plainly; FLAC compresses it the way a ZIP file does — smaller, but nothing thrown away, and unpacking gives you the original back exactly.

Lossy formats — MP3, AAC, Opus — achieve much smaller files by permanently discarding parts of the sound. They rely on the fact that human hearing misses a great deal: a quiet sound immediately after a loud one, very high frequencies, detail masked by other detail. The encoder identifies material you are unlikely to notice and deletes it.

For listening, a well-encoded lossy file is genuinely hard to distinguish from lossless. The trouble starts when you process it.

Why it matters for processing

This is the part most guides omit, and it is the reason to care at all.

Audio processing depends on fine detail — the subtle differences that distinguish one instrument from another, or a voice from a guitar occupying the same frequencies. Lossy encoding removed exactly that kind of detail, because it was judged inaudible in isolation.

So separation, noise reduction and pitch shifting all perform measurably better on lossless input. You are handing the tool a complete picture rather than a compressed approximation of one.

The practical rule: lossy for listening, lossless for working. If a file has further to travel, keep it lossless until the end.

Generation loss

The related trap, and one people fall into repeatedly.

Every time audio passes through a lossy encoder, more is discarded. Encode once at a decent bitrate and the result is fine. Take that MP3, edit it, export as MP3 again, and the second encoder is not compressing the original recording — it is compressing an already-degraded approximation, making fresh guesses on top of old ones.

Do this three or four times and it becomes clearly audible: smeared cymbals, a hollow quality in vocals, a loss of air at the top.

The fix is simply to keep intermediate steps lossless and encode once, at the very end. Cutting, joining and separating should all produce lossless output if the file is going anywhere else afterwards.

The formats, briefly

WAV — lossless, uncompressed, universally supported. Large files. The right choice for anything being edited or processed, and for handing audio to another application.

FLAC — lossless, compressed to roughly half the size of WAV. Identical quality. Excellent for archiving. Slightly less universally supported by consumer devices, though that gap has largely closed.

MP3 — lossy, ancient, and plays absolutely everywhere. Not the most efficient format any more, but its universality is unmatched. The safe default for sharing.

AAC / M4A — lossy, more efficient than MP3 at the same bitrate. The standard across Apple devices and most streaming. A better technical choice than MP3 where you know it will be supported.

OGG / Opus — lossy and genuinely excellent, particularly at low bitrates and for speech. Less universally supported on consumer hardware, which is its only real drawback.

Bitrate, in practical terms

Bitrate is how much data per second a lossy file is allowed. More data, more detail retained, larger file.

For music, 128 kbps is noticeably compromised on decent headphones — cymbals lose their shimmer first. 192 kbps is a reasonable middle ground where most people stop noticing. 320 kbps is the practical ceiling for MP3, and past it you are adding file size without adding much audible benefit.

For speech, far less is needed. Voice occupies a narrower range and tolerates compression well, so a podcast at 96 or 128 kbps is perfectly acceptable and keeps files small.

The thing to remember is that bitrate cannot restore anything. Converting a 128 kbps file to 320 kbps does not improve it — it produces a larger file containing the same degraded audio. Quality is set when material is first discarded.

Sample rate and channels

Two settings that matter less than people assume, with one exception each.

Sample rate is how many times per second the audio was measured. 44,100 Hz is the CD standard and entirely sufficient for listening; 48,000 Hz is standard for video. The exception: if your audio is going into a video project, match the project's rate to avoid conversion.

Channels — stereo carries two, mono carries one. The exception: for a speech recording made on a single microphone, converting to mono halves the file size with no loss whatsoever, because both channels contained the same thing. For music, keep stereo.

Choosing, in practice

Reduced to the decisions you will actually face:

Sending to a video editor? WAV, at the project's sample rate.

About to separate, cut or clean it? Start from the most lossless source you have; export WAV if there are more steps to come.

Archiving something you care about? FLAC — lossless, half the size of WAV.

Sharing or uploading? MP3 at 192 or 320, encoded once at the end.

A voice recording that is finished? Mono, and a modest bitrate. There is nothing to gain from more.

Not sure? WAV. You can always convert down later; you can never convert back up.

Why file sizes differ so much

It helps to have a rough sense of the numbers, because it explains most format decisions on its own.

A three-minute stereo song as WAV runs to roughly 30 MB. The same song as FLAC lands around half that, with identical quality. As a 320 kbps MP3 it is around 7 MB, and at 128 kbps roughly 3 MB.

So the jump from lossless to lossy saves a great deal, while the jump from 320 to 128 saves comparatively little in absolute terms and costs noticeably more quality. That is the argument for not going below 192 for music unless size is genuinely critical.

Speech is different. A one-hour interview at 320 kbps is pointlessly large; the same recording in mono at 96 kbps is a fraction of the size and sounds no worse, because voice simply does not need the bandwidth.

Metadata and file names

A small practical matter that causes disproportionate annoyance.

Converting between formats frequently drops embedded information — title, artist, album art. If a file matters to you, check the tags survived, particularly when converting for a device that organises music by them.

File names matter more than they should. Processing several files usually produces several downloads with near-identical names, and sorting them out afterwards is tedious. Name your sources clearly before you start, and rename outputs immediately rather than promising yourself you will do it later.

If you are producing a set of files that belong together, settle on one format and one naming pattern at the start. Mixing formats within a batch is a small decision that reliably creates confusion downstream.

What about streaming quality?

A question that comes up constantly, and the answer is more nuanced than the marketing suggests.

Streaming services deliver lossy audio, typically somewhere between 128 and 320 kbps depending on the service and your settings. Some now offer lossless tiers.

For listening, the difference between a good 256 kbps stream and lossless is genuinely difficult to hear on most equipment, in most rooms, at most listening volumes. Anyone insisting otherwise is usually describing ideal conditions rather than normal ones.

For processing, the difference is real and easy to demonstrate. This is why separating a track you own as a proper file gives a better result than separating something captured from a stream — and why source quality is the first thing to check when a result disappoints.

Common questions

Can I improve a low-quality MP3 by converting it?

No. The discarded information is gone permanently. Conversion changes the container, not the content.

Is FLAC actually identical to WAV?

Yes, bit for bit, once decoded. The compression is entirely reversible.

Which format should I use for separation?

Whatever lossless copy you have. If your only source is an MP3, use the highest bitrate version you can find — the difference in the result is easy to hear.

Does converting between lossy formats lose quality?

Yes, and more than people expect. Going MP3 to AAC decodes and re-encodes, discarding material twice. Avoid it where you can.
