How to Make Your Own Backing Track for Free

July 1, 2026 · 8 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

There is a particular frustration in needing a backing track for a song nobody has made one for. You search, you find the same three karaoke channels covering the same few hundred hits, and the song you actually need — a film song, a regional track, an album cut, anything recent — simply is not there. Making your own takes about a minute. Making a good one takes about ten, and this is what those ten minutes go on.

Start with the right file

Everything downstream depends on this, and it is the step people skip.

Separation works by identifying subtle differences between instruments. A heavily compressed file has already discarded exactly the fine detail those differences live in — an encoder throws away material it judges you will not consciously miss, and that material is precisely what the engine needs.

Feed it a low-bitrate download and the instrumental comes out thin and slightly smeared. Feed it a proper file and the difference is not subtle.

So before anything else: find the best copy of the song you legitimately have. This single choice matters more than every other decision in this article combined.

Trim before you separate

Most backing tracks do not need the whole song.

A school performance might need one verse and one chorus. A practice loop might need eight bars. Cutting first is faster, it costs fewer credits since cost scales with length, and cutting is free and unlimited anyway.

Use the Audio Cutter and place your cuts in quiet moments — the gap between phrases, the breath before a chorus. Cutting mid-note produces a click, and a very short fade removes it if you have no quiet moment available.

One thing to get right: start a beat or two before the vocal enters. A backing track that begins exactly on the first sung word gives the singer nothing to come in on, and live that is the difference between a confident entry and a fumbled one.

Check the vocal stem, not just the instrumental

This is the trick that saves the most time, and almost nobody does it.

Listen to the isolated vocal first. If it sounds clean and complete, your instrumental is clean too. If you can hear cymbals or guitar bleeding into the vocal, then that same material is now missing from the instrumental, and you have learned in twenty seconds what would otherwise take a full playthrough to notice.

It is a diagnostic, not a deliverable — you may never use the vocal file at all.

Fix the key while you are here

A backing track in the wrong key is worse than no backing track, because it trains tension.

Songs are recorded in whatever suited the original singer, and there is no reason that should suit you. If the chorus sits two notes above where you are comfortable, shift the whole song down instead of straining through it.

The Pitch Shifter is free and unlimited, and it lets you hear the change while the track plays — so you can find the right key by ear rather than by guessing at numbers.

Move in small steps; one or two semitones solves most problems. Push past four or five and the backing starts sounding artificial. And check the verse as well as the chorus, because dropping the chorus into comfort can push the verse below where your voice has any body.

What to expect from the result

Being realistic here prevents a lot of disappointment.

On a cleanly produced modern song, the instrumental will be good enough that most listeners would assume it was officially released.

Backing vocals and harmonies will leave with the lead, because they are voices. On songs built around layered vocals the instrumental will feel noticeably emptier — that is inherent, not a fault.

Vocal reverb was printed into the mix, so on songs with heavy reverb a faint ghost of the voice may remain. Singing over it covers this more effectively than reprocessing.

Dense live recordings separate worst. Crowd noise and stage bleed blur the boundaries the engine relies on.

Preparing it for actual use

A few practical things once you have the file.

Test it on the system it will play through. An instrumental that sounds full on headphones can sound thin through a hall PA, and finding that out on the day is unpleasant.

If you need several songs, join them into one file with the free Song Joiner. One file to press play on is far less stressful than juggling four during a performance.

Match levels if you do join them. Tracks from different albums sit at different loudness, and a set that jumps in volume between songs is distracting.

Export lossless if the file is going anywhere else, and encode to MP3 only at the very end if you need a smaller file.

Songs that will not cooperate

Some material resists separation no matter what you do, and recognising it early saves a wasted afternoon.

Live concert recordings are the hardest case. Crowd noise, stage bleed and room reflections blur exactly the boundaries the engine depends on, and the instrumental usually arrives muddy.

Very old recordings — anything from the 1960s or earlier — were often mixed in ways that scatter instruments oddly, or captured in mono to begin with. Separation still works, but expect artefacts.

Songs where the voice is heavily processed are genuinely ambiguous. A vocoder or extreme pitch manipulation blurs the line between voice and instrument, and if a human listener cannot say where one ends, no engine will either.

In each of these cases the answer is usually to choose a different song rather than to keep reprocessing. Reprocessing an already-separated file compounds artefacts instead of clearing them.

A quick workflow to reuse

Once you have done this a few times it collapses into a routine worth writing down.

Find the best copy of the song. Trim to the section you need, starting a beat or two before the vocal enters. Separate. Check the vocal stem to confirm the separation was clean. Shift the key if needed, auditioning while it plays. Test on the system it will actually play through.

Six steps, roughly ten minutes, and the result is a backing track built for your voice rather than someone else's.

A note on where you can use it

Making a backing track from a song you own, for practice or a private event, is uncontroversial.

Publishing it — uploading the instrumental, selling it, or using it in monetised content — means distributing a derivative of someone else's recording, and that needs permission. Our copyright guide covers the distinction properly.

Using it for something other than singing

Backing tracks get made for reasons beyond karaoke, and the process is identical.

Dance and fitness instructors strip the vocal so counting and cues are audible over the music, which makes a class considerably easier to run.

Instrumentalists build tracks to play against — a guitarist practising a solo, a drummer working on a groove. Removing your own instrument rather than isolating it is the better exercise, because you have to hold your part without a reference.

School and event organisers need music underneath a speech or a presentation, where a sung vocal would compete with the speaker.

Content creators need beds for videos where the original vocal would clash with narration.

In every case the recipe is the same: best available source, trim first, separate, adjust key if a singer is involved.

Common questions

Why does my instrumental sound hollow?

Almost always the source file. Try a better copy of the same song before concluding the song will not work.

Can I remove only the lead and keep harmonies?

Not reliably. Separation treats voices as a category, and harmonies are voices.

How long does it take?

Usually under a minute for a typical song, depending on length and the device you are on. The very first run takes longer while the engine gets itself ready; everything after that starts immediately.
