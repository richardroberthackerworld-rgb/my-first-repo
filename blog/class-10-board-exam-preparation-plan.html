Class 10 Board Exams: A 90-Day Preparation Plan That Works

July 19, 2026 · 11 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

Three months is enough to move from average to excellent — if every week has a job to do. The students who jump grades in the last ninety days are rarely the ones who suddenly study more hours; they are the ones who stop studying vaguely and start following a plan where each phase has a clear, single purpose.

The Class 10 boards feel enormous because everything seems to matter at once — every subject, every chapter, all urgent. That formless pressure is exactly what causes students to spin their wheels, re-reading favourite chapters while weak ones rot. The cure is structure. Below is a ninety-day plan broken into four phases, each with one job. Do not try to do everything every day; do the job of the phase you are in, and trust the sequence.

Days 1–30: cover the syllabus

The job of the first month is coverage, and nothing more ambitious than that. Go chapter by chapter through every subject, textbook first, and set yourself one simple, non-perfectionist goal: that no chapter is completely unknown to you. You are not trying to master anything yet — you are drawing the map, making sure there are no blank regions you will panic about in February.

As you go, make one page of notes per chapter, in your own handwriting — the key formulas, definitions and diagrams, condensed onto a single side. Do not underestimate this. The act of deciding what belongs on that one page forces you to process the chapter rather than passively read it, so the notes are half the learning. And the pages themselves become pure gold in the final week, when you will revise fifteen chapters from fifteen sheets instead of reopening the whole textbook. Handwritten, by you, one page each — this habit quietly carries the entire plan.

Days 31–60: practice by chapter

The job of the second month is the great switch — from reading to solving. This is the phase most students skip, and skipping it is why they read for months and still underperform. Passive reading builds recognition; the exam demands production. For each chapter, take a fresh question paper — a mix of MCQs, short answers and long answers — and actually write the answers out with a pen, under a time limit, as if it were the real thing.

Finding enough fresh papers used to be the bottleneck here, but you can generate chapter-wise papers free on 7Marks and then mark yourself honestly against the answer key. Honesty is the whole game at this stage — the marks you fudge now are the marks you lose in March. And alongside the marking, keep an error notebook: every wrong answer gets exactly one line noting the question type and the mistake you made. Do this for a couple of weeks and something clarifying happens: most students discover that around 80% of their lost marks come from the same five repeated mistakes. Once you can see those five, revision stops being a fog and becomes a to-do list.

Days 61–85: full papers, exam conditions

The job of this phase is to turn chapter-level competence into exam-level performance, which is a different and specific skill. Knowing the material is necessary but not sufficient; you also have to sustain focus for three hours, manage your time across a whole paper, and keep your nerve. That only comes from full-length rehearsal.

  • Sit two full-length papers per subject, each a proper three hours, with no phone anywhere in the room. The phone rule is not moralising — a single glance fractures the sustained concentration the real exam requires, and you are training that concentration as much as the content.
  • Alternate subjects day by day so nothing goes cold. Boards test everything in a compressed window, so your revision should keep all subjects warm rather than finishing one and abandoning it.
  • After each paper, spend as long reviewing it as you spent writing it. This is the highest-value hour in the whole plan and the one most people rush. The paper tells you precisely where you are still leaking marks; a full review turns those leaks into fixes, while a quick glance at the score teaches you nothing.

The last 5 days

The job of the final week is calm consolidation, not last-minute cramming, and the discipline to do less is what separates a strong finish from a shaky one. No new topics — starting something fresh now only manufactures anxiety and displaces sleep. Read only two things: your one-page chapter notes and your error notebook. Between them, they are a distilled version of everything you learned and everything you personally get wrong, which is exactly what you want in front of you.

And sleep seven to eight hours a night, deliberately. This is not soft advice; it is strategy. Memory consolidates during sleep, so the revision you did today is only made permanent by the night that follows it. A tired brain in the exam hall loses more marks through careless errors and blanking than any single unrevised chapter ever would. The student who sleeps well in the final week walks in sharper than the one who traded those hours for another read-through.

When you get stuck

One last principle that runs through all ninety days: guard your time against single doubts. It is astonishing how often a preparation plan is derailed not by laziness but by one problem that a student wrestles with for a whole evening and still does not crack. That lost evening was supposed to cover a chapter, and now it has not.

So do not let any single doubt eat your schedule. When you are genuinely stuck, snap a photo of the question into 7Solve, read the step-by-step solution, and then — importantly — immediately try a similar question yourself to confirm you have actually understood rather than just nodded along. This keeps you moving. Stuck-time is the silent killer of preparation plans; the students who finish the syllabus are not the ones who never get stuck, but the ones who get unstuck fast and keep the plan on schedule.
