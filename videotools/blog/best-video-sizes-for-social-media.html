The Right Video Size for Every Platform — a 2026 Cheat Sheet

By VidLab·10 min read·Updated 2026

Post a widescreen video to Reels and it appears as a tiny letterboxed strip marooned in the middle of the screen. Post a vertical video to YouTube's main feed and it's pillar-boxed with black bars swallowing two-thirds of the frame. Neither looks intentional, and viewers read "wrong size" as "amateur" in a fraction of a second. Getting aspect ratio right is the cheapest way to look professional — and the fastest way to look sloppy if you skip it.

This is the complete, current cheat sheet: the exact ratios and resolutions each platform wants, why they differ, what actually happens when you get it wrong, and — the part that saves the most time — how to reframe a single video for every platform without reshooting or paying for anything.

The only three ratios you need to know

Behind a dozen platforms and their endless format names, there are really only three shapes of video, and everything is a variation on them.

  • 16:9 — landscape (wide). The classic television and monitor shape. This is the home format of the YouTube watch page, and the right choice for anything watched on a big screen, tutorials, and long-form content.
  • 9:16 — portrait (tall). The full-screen phone shape. This is the native format of Shorts, Reels, TikTok and Stories — the entire short-form vertical ecosystem. If you make one kind of video for reach in 2026, it's this one.
  • 1:1 — square. The compromise shape. It fills more of a phone feed than 16:9 without committing to full vertical, which historically made it the safe pick for the Instagram and Facebook feed. It's used less now that vertical dominates, but it still travels well across feeds.
The mental model: big screen → 16:9. Phone, full-screen → 9:16. Mixed feed where you're not sure → 1:1 or 4:5. Pick the shape by where it will be watched, not by where you're uploading it.

The platform-by-platform cheat sheet

Here is what each surface actually wants. Aim for these and your video fills the frame the way the platform intends.

  • YouTube (main video): 16:9, ideally 1920×1080 or higher. This is the one place widescreen is still king.
  • YouTube Shorts: 9:16, 1080×1920. Vertical, full-screen, under 60 seconds.
  • Instagram Reels & TikTok: 9:16, 1080×1920. The dominant short-form format.
  • Instagram feed: 4:5 (portrait, 1080×1350) fills the most feed space and is the current sweet spot; 1:1 square (1080×1080) is the safe classic.
  • Instagram & Facebook Stories: 9:16, 1080×1920.
  • Facebook feed: 1:1 or 4:5 for the feed; 16:9 for shared links and longer video.
  • X (Twitter): 16:9 or 1:1 both work; 16:9 for landscape clips, 1:1 to take more feed height.
  • LinkedIn: 1:1 or 16:9; square performs well in the professional feed.
  • Pinterest: 2:3 or 9:16 vertical — this is a tall-format platform.

Resolution: how many pixels is enough

Aspect ratio is the shape; resolution is the detail. They're independent — a video can be 9:16 at 720×1280 or 9:16 at 1080×1920, same shape, different sharpness. For 2026, the practical targets are simple:

  • 1080p is the baseline. That's 1920×1080 for landscape or 1080×1920 for vertical. Every platform accepts it, it looks crisp on any phone, and it uploads quickly.
  • 4K is worth it only sometimes. Shoot and edit in 4K if viewers will watch full-screen on a TV or monitor, or if you want room to crop and reframe without losing sharpness. For a video that lives entirely on phones, 4K mostly costs you file size and upload time for detail nobody sees.
  • 720p is the floor. It's acceptable for quick, casual clips and for keeping files tiny, but it starts to look soft on modern high-density phone screens. Don't go below it if you can help it.

One counterintuitive point: every platform re-compresses whatever you upload. So uploading a pristine, lightly-compressed 1080p file gives the platform's encoder better source material to work from than a heavily-squeezed file — even though the final result is squeezed either way. Upload quality in; let the platform do the shrinking.

What goes wrong when the ratio is off

When your video's shape doesn't match the surface, the platform does one of two ugly things. It either letterboxes your video — shrinking it and padding the empty space with black or a blurred backdrop — or it crops it, chopping off the sides or top and bottom to force a fit, often slicing straight through a face or the text you carefully placed.

A wrongly-sized video doesn't just look worse — it performs worse, because the algorithm reads the wasted space as a weaker, less-native piece of content.

The subtler cost is momentum. Feeds reward video that feels native to the platform. A letterboxed clip on a full-screen vertical feed signals "this was made for somewhere else," and both viewers and ranking systems respond to that. Matching the ratio isn't cosmetic; it's part of how far the video travels.

You don't need to shoot separate versions. The efficient workflow is to edit once and re-export in each shape you need. Safe zones: leaving room for the UI

The last mistake is placing your content where the app's own buttons cover it. On a full-screen vertical video, the platform stacks a caption, a username, a row of like/comment/share icons, and sometimes a music credit — all over your footage. Put your text or your subject there and it's buried.

The fix is to respect the safe zone: keep anything essential — captions, logos, key action — within the central band of the frame, clear of the bottom quarter and the right edge where the interface lives. When you design a vertical video, picture the buttons that will sit on top of it and leave them room. It's the difference between text that reads and text that fights a "Follow" button for space.

Get the shape right, hit at least 1080p, upload clean source, and keep your content out of the UI's way — that's the whole game. Do it once deliberately and it becomes automatic, and every video you post afterward looks like it belongs exactly where you put it.
