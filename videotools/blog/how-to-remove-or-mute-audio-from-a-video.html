How to Remove or Mute Audio from a Video — Free

By VidLab·7 min read·Updated 2026

Sometimes the picture is perfect but the sound is the problem — a phone conversation in the background, wind roaring across the microphone, a copyrighted song playing in a shop, or simply your own voice saying something you'd rather nobody heard. Whatever the reason, stripping the audio out of a video is one of the most common and useful edits there is, and it's also one of the easiest to do badly, because a lot of tools re-compress the whole video just to silence it. Done right, you can remove or mute audio in seconds with zero loss to the picture.

This guide covers the whole subject: the difference between muting and removing audio, when to silence just part of a clip, how removing audio helps you dodge copyright claims, keeping your video quality intact, and doing all of it free in your browser with nothing uploaded.

Why remove audio in the first place

There are more reasons than people expect, and knowing yours points you at the right fix:

  • Bad ambient sound — wind, traffic, a noisy room, a fan — that ruins otherwise good footage.
  • Private conversations caught in the background that you don't want to publish.
  • Copyrighted music playing in the environment that could trigger a claim on upload.
  • You plan to add your own audio — a voiceover or music track — and want to clear the original first.
  • The video is meant to be silent — a background loop, a GIF-style clip, a store display — where sound would be intrusive.
Point yourself at the right fix: if you want the video totally silent, remove the audio. If you want to swap in new sound, remove the old and add yours. If only one stretch is the problem, silence just that section rather than the whole clip.

Mute, remove, or replace?

Three subtly different outcomes, and it's worth knowing which you want. Muting sets the volume to zero but leaves an (empty) audio track in the file — fine in most cases, and reversible if you change your mind mid-edit. Removing strips the audio track out entirely, producing a truly silent video and a slightly smaller file. Replacing means removing the original audio and adding new sound in its place — the most common goal, since a silent video often wants music or narration to fill the gap.

For most people the practical answer is: remove the original completely if the sound is unusable, then decide whether the video works silent or needs new audio. A totally silent video can feel eerie or unfinished, so unless silence is the point, plan to add something in its place.

Silencing just part of a video

Often the whole soundtrack is fine except for one stretch — a swear word, a phone ringing, a bystander's comment, a burst of copyrighted music. Muting the entire video to fix ten seconds is overkill and loses all your good audio. Instead, isolate the problem section: split the clip on both sides of the bad audio, then mute or remove the sound on just that middle piece while the rest plays normally. This targeted approach — the same "split twice and treat the middle" logic you'd use to cut out a visual moment — keeps everything good and silences only what needs it.

Removing audio to avoid copyright claims

One of the most practical reasons to strip audio is dodging automated copyright claims. Platforms scan the sound of your upload against a database of registered music; if a song is playing in the background of your clip — even faintly, even unintentionally — it can match and trigger a claim that mutes, blocks, or demonetizes your video. Removing the original audio removes the fingerprint, so there's nothing to match. Strip the environmental sound, then add cleared, royalty-free music in its place, and you get a video that sounds intentional and sails past the copyright scanners. (Our guide to adding music covers where to find tracks you can safely use.)

If a copyrighted song is playing anywhere in your footage, the safest move is simple: remove the original audio entirely and replace it with music you're cleared to use.

Keeping the video quality

Here's the trap: many tools re-encode the entire video just to change the audio, which needlessly re-compresses your picture and costs a little quality every time. Removing or muting audio should never touch the video stream. A good tool keeps the video data exactly as it was and simply drops or silences the sound, which is both instant and pixel-perfect. So when you remove audio, make sure the picture stays untouched — there's no reason a silence should ever soften your footage.
