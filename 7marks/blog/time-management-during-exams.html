Time Management in the Exam Hall: Finish Every Paper

July 4, 2026 · 10 min read · by Shirandasu Sandeep

Thousands of marks are lost every year not to ignorance but to the clock — students who knew the answers but never reached the questions, or reached them with two minutes left. Beating the clock is a separate, learnable skill from knowing the material, and it is the one almost nobody practises.

Here is a scene that plays out in every exam hall: a capable student walks out devastated, not because the paper was too hard, but because they left an entire section blank. They knew that section. They simply ran out of time on the earlier questions and never got there. Those are the cruellest marks to lose, because they were fully earnable — lost to pacing, not to ignorance. This article is about making sure that student is never you.

Budget before you write

The first two minutes of any exam should be spent doing arithmetic, not answers, and this feels counterintuitive when adrenaline is screaming at you to start writing. Resist it. Take the total time, subtract about ten minutes for a final review, and divide what remains across the sections in proportion to their marks. A three-hour, eighty-mark paper works out to roughly two minutes per mark — a number worth knowing before you walk in.

Then make the plan visible: write your section deadlines on the rough sheet. "Section B by 11:20, Section C by 12:05." This tiny act is what makes time management actually work, because it turns an invisible, creeping problem into a visible one. Without written checkpoints, you drift — falling behind so gradually that you do not notice until it is too late to recover. With them, you glance up, see you are five minutes behind at the Section B checkpoint, and correct course while correcting is still cheap. Drift caught early is a nudge; drift caught late is a disaster.

The three-pass method

How you move through the paper matters as much as your time budget, and the single best approach is to go through it in three deliberate passes rather than ploughing straight down in order.

  1. Pass 1 — harvest. Go through the whole paper answering everything you find easy, in order, without pausing to wrestle with anything hard. This does two crucial things at once: it banks a pile of secure marks quickly, and it settles your nerves. Every easy question answered is money in the bank and a small dose of calm, and calm is itself worth marks on the questions still to come.
  2. Pass 2 — work. Now return to the moderate questions — the ones you can do but that need real thought — and give them the bulk of your remaining time. By this point you are warmed up, calmer, and you already know how much time you have left for them, because the easy marks are secured.
  3. Pass 3 — fight. Whatever time is left goes to the hardest questions. And even here, write something: even partial steps earn partial marks in most exams, so a formula and a first line of working is almost always worth more than a blank.

Notice what this method makes impossible. The classic exam-hall catastrophe — sinking twenty-five minutes into a stubborn question 2 while three easy questions further down go completely unread — simply cannot happen when you harvest the whole paper first. You will have already banked those easy marks before the hard question ever got its claws into you.

Rules for the stubborn question

Every paper has at least one question designed to swallow your time. A few firm rules keep it from doing so.

  • Set a hard per-question ceiling — marks times two minutes is a good default — and honour it. When you hit the ceiling and the answer still is not coming, mark the question, leave a gap, and move on. You can return in pass three. The sunk-cost feeling ("but I've already spent so long on this") is exactly the trap; walk away.
  • Never erase large sections of work. If you change your mind, strike a single neat line through it and carry on. Examiners mark your best attempt, and frantically erasing wastes precious minutes and can rub out something that would have earned partial credit.
  • Attempt everything. This is the simplest rule and the most violated. A blank scores exactly zero, guaranteed. A relevant formula and a first step usually score something. Over a whole paper, those "somethings" on questions you thought you couldn't do add up to a grade.

Train it before you need it

Here is the part that ties it all together, and the part most students skip: exam-hall pacing is muscle memory, and muscle memory is built only by rehearsal. You cannot read about the three-pass method the night before and expect it to run smoothly under real pressure. It has to be practised until it is automatic, so that on the day your attention is free for the questions, not for managing yourself.

So rehearse the whole thing under realistic conditions. Generate a full-length paper on 7Marks, set a genuine three-hour timer, put your phone outside the room, and sit it properly — budgeting, three passes, checkpoints and all. Do this once a week in the run-up to the exam. By the time the real day arrives, the clock will not be a terrifying stranger; it will feel like an old opponent you have already beaten five times over. That familiarity — knowing exactly how the three hours feel, how fast two minutes per mark goes, when to move on — is what lets your knowledge actually reach the answer sheet.
